## Executive Summary

The summary is broadly accurate: fusion research primarily targets deuterium–tritium reactions using magnetic or inertial confinement. Major scientific milestones have occurred, but reliable, economical, continuous fusion electricity has not yet been demonstrated.

---

## Key Findings

- Tokamaks and stellarators use magnetic fields to confine plasma; laser-driven inertial confinement compresses fuel rapidly.
- Plasma stability and sustained net energy gain remain important challenges, although experiments have achieved notable fusion-energy milestones.
- A power plant must address neutron damage, component lifetime, heat exhaust, maintenance, and tritium breeding and handling.
- Deuterium is abundant, but tritium is scarce and would need to be produced in lithium-containing breeding blankets for large-scale deployment.
- Commercial operation remains unresolved; experimental fusion gain is not equivalent to net electricity at the plant level.
- The confidence score of 0.96 is reasonable for the broad overview, but specific milestone claims would require cited experiments, dates, and definitions of gain.

## Fact Check Notes

The claims are generally factually sound but lack citations and quantitative context. “Net energy gain” should distinguish plasma or target gain from whole-facility energy gain. “Primarily deuterium and tritium” is accurate for near-term fusion development, while other fuels such as deuterium–helium-3 or proton–boron are less mature. The statement that laser-driven inertial confinement has achieved milestones is accurate, but those demonstrations have not established continuous power production or commercial viability. The list of challenges is valid but omits economic competitiveness, reactor availability, tritium regulation, and efficient electricity conversion.

---

## Final Conclusion

The submission provides a reliable high-level overview. Its central conclusion—that fusion has made substantial progress but continuous, commercially viable power remains unproven—is accurate. Stronger validation would require named experiments, numerical results, dates, and authoritative sources.
